\textbf{Major points:}

\bigskip

\noindent
0. The authors should work out the scientific contribution of the article more explicitly. On first glance, the authors appear to report on computational experiments only with what seems like a simple extension of what is available already. The work at hand should be put in perspective, discussing both the difficulties and the means to overcome them. Moreover, the findings should be stated in a realistic and modest way (see item 1).

\emph{Response:} We agree that this general point is central, and we have addressed it throughout the revised manuscript and in several of the detailed responses below. In particular, we have reduced the claimed generality of the manuscript, clarified the distinction between the computed effective crack resistance and a homogenized fracture toughness, and stated more explicitly which assumptions are not verified in the present study.

Concerning the scientific contribution, the reviewer is right that one part of the work is an extension of the previously used phase-field fracture framework to account for J2-plasticity. However, the consequences of this extension for the proposed numerical experiment are not obvious a priori, and, to the best of our knowledge, this comparison has not previously been carried out for the considered effective-crack-resistance setting. The results show that matching the one-dimensional tensile strength alone does not reproduce the crack resistance obtained with the ductile material law; the additional dissipation during plastic deformation and crack re-nucleation plays a decisive role. Similarly, the three-dimensional extension is not meant as a formal homogenization proof, but as a numerical test of whether the toughening and saturation effects observed in the two-dimensional setting persist when the crack front has an additional degree of freedom. The revised manuscript now presents these points more explicitly and more modestly.

We also agree that the approach lacks strict mathematical proof of a homogenized fracture toughness and that representativity is not fully established. These limitations are now stated directly in the introduction, in the definition of the numerical experiment, and in the conclusion. For details, we refer in particular to our responses to points 1, 2, 7, 8, 9, 12, and 22.

2. Large parts of the introduction are misleading. There is no meaningful "homogenized fracture toughness". From a mathematical point of view, there should be a useful and naturally emerging "effective material model" at the macroscopic scale. Yet, this is not clear to the best of my knowledge - do micro-cracks coalesce to a "macro-crack", governed by an anisotropic elasticity tensor and an anisotropic fracture toughness? I do not know. \emph{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. The existence of a homogenized fracture toughness in the same sense as homogenized elastic or plastic properties is still an active research question, and our previous wording did not make this limitation sufficiently clear. We also agree that the definition by Hossain et al. should not be presented as a classical homogenization result or as proof of a material property obtained in an RVE limit.

We have therefore revised the introduction and the section introducing the numerical experiment. The manuscript now explicitly states that it is not generally established that fracture in a heterogeneous material gives rise to a macroscopic effective material model with a well-defined fracture toughness. We also clarify that the approach of Hossain et al. is used here in an operational, experiment-like sense: a region containing microstructural features is forced to fracture, and the energetic driving force required for this process is measured. Such a value can only be representative if the resulting fracture pattern is itself representative of the material and loading situation.

We further state explicitly that the present study does not demonstrate convergence with respect to the size of the resolved porous domain, nor does it test different initial crack locations, crack-growth directions, or microstructure realizations. Accordingly, the results are now framed as a controlled numerical experiment on the influence of J2-plasticity for the selected simplified porous microstructure, rather than as the determination of a generally valid homogenized fracture toughness.

\noindent
6. Which software, which numerical parameters, which time-stepping scheme, which implementation, which hardware is used? Without this data, the results are neither understandable nor reproducible.

\emph{Response:} We agree that the numerical implementation was described too briefly. We have therefore added a new subsection before the parameter sets that summarizes the implementation and discretization details. The revised manuscript now states that the simulations were carried out with FEniCSx, more specifically \texttt{dolfinx} version 0.7.4, using a monolithic nonlinear solve. The nonlinear problem is solved with the PETSc-based Newton solver provided by FEniCSx, using the residual and tangent of the monolithic formulation. The linear subproblems are solved with the default PETSc Krylov solver GMRES and the default PETSc ILU preconditioner used by this setup.

We also added the time discretization and adaptive time-stepping strategy. The phase-field evolution equation is treated implicitly by a backward Euler scheme. The time step is reduced by a factor of two if Newton's method does not converge within eight iterations and is increased by a factor of two if convergence is reached in fewer than four iterations. The manuscript already provided the specific mesh sizes and phase-field length scales in the individual simulation setups; we now additionally state the general discretization rule that first-order triangular elements are used in two dimensions, first-order tetrahedral elements in three dimensions, and that the mesh along the expected crack path is refined such that $\Delta h \leq \epsilon/2$, usually with a much smaller element size as specified in the respective sections.

12. I wondered about the embedding of the "representative region" in a homogeneous "effective" material. Shouldn't the effective material be cubic in case a single spherical inclusion is used?

\emph{Response:} The reviewer is correct that the unit cell used to determine the effective stiffness is cubic in the three-dimensional case. Each spherical pore is centered in a cubic cell of edge length $w_c=w_s+L$, and the effective stiffness parameters are computed from this cubic unit cell.

The surrounding homogeneous domain, however, is a different object. It is not a unit cell, but a larger embedding domain used to apply the surfing boundary conditions around the resolved $6\times3\times2$ array of porous unit cells. This embedding domain is cuboidal rather than cubic, but it is assigned the effective stiffness obtained from the cubic porous unit cell. We have added a short clarification in Section 5 to make this distinction explicit.

\emph{Remark 2.1:} The J-integral is not path-independent when the crack surface is not a straight line or when the material is elastically heterogeneous. Are the authors computing it ``far enough'' so that the non-planarity of the crack or the pores do not impact J?

\emph{Response:} We agree with the reviewer that the crack pattern and its interaction with the resolved microstructure, including heterogeneities and non-planar or tortuous crack paths, clearly affect the value of the J-Integral recorded at the boundary of the explicitly modelled microstructure. This is precisely why the boundary value is interpreted as the energetic driving force required for the particular crack-growth process in the numerical experiment.

The point we rely on is slightly different: the homogeneous material surrounding the explicitly resolved microstructure is assumed not to alter the recorded J-Integral. In other words, the value evaluated at the boundary of the explicitly modelled microstructure should coincide with the value obtained at the boundary of a larger embedding region assigned representative homogeneous properties. This behavior was also observed by Hossain et al. in their original study, although we have not explicitly repeated such a contour/enlargement study here. We have added a clarifying sentence to the manuscript.

\emph{Related changes:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Page 5: \added{The recorded value is, of course, affected by the crack pattern inside the explicitly resolved microstructure; however, following the observation reported by Hossain et al., the additional homogeneous surrounding material is assumed not to change the value of the boundary J-Integral, so that evaluating it at the boundary of the resolved microstructure or at the boundary of an embedding region with representative effective properties gives the same macroscopic driving force.}
\end{itemize}

\emph{Remark 2.3:} The authors may be interest by a new method called VSBC (Variable stiffness boundary condition), proposed by M.~Z.~Hossain (Hao et al., CMAME, 2025, doi:10.1016/j.cma.2025.118414).

\emph{Response:} We thank the reviewer for pointing us to this recent contribution. The variable-stiffness boundary condition proposed by Hao et al. is highly relevant for the broader context of numerical and experimental approaches to determine effective fracture properties. In contrast to the surfing boundary condition used in the present manuscript, VSBC translates a simple uniform remote displacement into a non-uniform load through an engineered stiffness gradient, which makes the method more accessible for experimental setups and commercial finite-element software. We have added this contribution to the introduction. In the present study, however, we keep the boundary-condition formulation used in the original approach by Hossain et al.; a systematic comparison with VSBC would require additional simulations and is beyond the scope of the present manuscript.

\emph{Related changes:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Introduction: \added{A recent related development is the variable stiffness boundary condition of Hao et al., which builds on the surfing boundary condition but translates a simple uniform remote displacement into a non-uniform load through an engineered stiffness gradient, thereby facilitating implementation in experiments and commercial finite-element software.}
\item Bibliography: \added{Added Hao, Piel and Hossain (2026).}
\end{itemize}

\emph{Response:} We agree with the reviewer that the crack-tip position during a pinning phase requires clarification. \emph{Remark 5.2:} Can the authors comment on the occurrence of instabilities during crack growth in the 3D simulations? The behavior appears smoother/less intermittent but it may be just a misinterpretation of Fig. 18.

\emph{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. The crack propagation in the three-dimensional simulations is indeed smoother than in the two-dimensional case. This is caused by the additional degree of freedom of the crack front: in three dimensions, the crack front can propagate between pores and is therefore not trapped completely by a pore as in the two-dimensional plane strain setting. This smoother behavior is visible both in the crack-tip evolution and in the J-Integral response.

However, the propagation is not fully smooth. Fig.~17 still shows phases of nearly instantaneous crack advance, and the peaks in Fig.~18 indicate that the phases in which the crack front is forced to pass between pores still require an increased energetic driving force. As summarized in Fig.~20, the resulting toughening remains comparable in magnitude to the two-dimensional case, although the three-dimensional response is less intermittent. We have added this clarification to the manuscript.
